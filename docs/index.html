Living PRD (Product Requirements Document)

Updated every week as we test with real users and learn what works.

1. Target User & The Job to Be Done

Who is this for? Me first, as a Stern MBA Class of 2027 student recruiting off-cycle for full-time roles and programs. After that, friends and classmates who are running the same off-cycle search, all of whom I can reach in class or over text. Since Build 2, a classmate can clone the repository, run two commands and finish setup in the browser, so they no longer need me to edit files for them.

The painful "Before" workflow: Since August, I have seen, applied, researched, and interviewed for different roles across different industries. While each company posts its roles in a different place, the check is the same every week: open 12twenty, LinkedIn and each company's career page one by one, then read every description to see whether it wants an MBA, a 2027 start, or five more years of experience than I have. Most of that time goes to roles I could never get, and a program with a short application window can open and close between two of my checks.

The "After" workflow with this product: I keep one watchlist of companies and one profile built from my resume. A single click checks every board on the list, filters out roles that fail my rules on title, location, track and posting age, and scores the rest against my profile. What comes back is a ranked list with one sentence on why each role fits, the biggest gap, any dealbreaker, and a flag on what is new since my last check, which means I only open the postings worth my time. The first time, a setup screen asks for my resume, the kinds of roles and cities I want, the sectors of companies to watch, my schools and whether to score with AI or for free. Below the two columns, "Beyond your list" runs the same filters over roughly 9,000 more companies, so a startup I have never heard of can still reach me.

2. Scope & Explicit Non-Goals

  • In Scope (Core Workflow): Check my list. Pull every relevant open role from the Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, Workday and SmartRecruiters boards of the companies I choose, filter them by my rules, score the survivors against my resume, and show them in two columns: 2027 and new grad roles, and other open full-time roles. Setup in the browser and "Beyond your list" support this workflow rather than replace it.
  • Out of Scope #1: Auto-applying or writing cover letters. Shortlist decides where my time goes, but it never submits anything on my behalf.
  • Out of Scope #2: LinkedIn, Handshake and Stern's 12twenty. They sit behind logins, so this version stays with public job board APIs. The "Reach out" links on each role are plain LinkedIn searches that I open myself while logged in, so nothing about anyone is fetched or stored.
  • Out of Scope #3: Companies that run their own careers systems, such as Google, Amazon, Tesla and TikTok, and those on Eightfold, such as Microsoft and Applied Materials. Each would need its own connector, and none of them offer a documented public job feed.

3. How It Works Under the Hood & Riskiest Assumption (Week 2+)

Riskiest Assumption: That a model's fit score lines up with my own judgment closely enough that I trust the ranking instead of opening every posting myself. If the scores feel random, the tool saves no time. Build 1 already showed that keyword matching alone fails this test (see the build log), so the AI scorer is the core of the product rather than an add-on. Build 2 gave the first evidence that the model reads the resume rather than matching words. When a test resume was loaded by mistake, every reason cited that person's work history, and with my own resume back, the same kinds of roles were explained through my manufacturing, supplier and real estate experience.

Simple Architecture:

[Setup in the browser: resume, roles, cities, sectors, schools] → [Watchlist of 238 companies, plus about 9,000 more to explore] → [Local Node.js app] → [Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, Workday and SmartRecruiters] → [Rule-based filter, free, with a reason for every drop] → [Claude Haiku 5.5 via OpenRouter, one call per new role, capped per check, scores saved] → [Two columns, plus Beyond your list]

Build 1 · Intent.md & Throwaway Prototype (Due Nov 9)

  • What shipped: A local prototype of the full core workflow (screenshot above). The first live run checked 9 companies, pulled 8,860 open postings, passed 353 through the filter, and scored 40 for $0.0078. Before the deadline, I scaled it from 9 companies to a list of about 300, which meant adding two more job board systems, Workday and SmartRecruiters, that many large employers use. A discovery command now finds each company's board from its name or a pasted careers link, and all 241 boards were reachable on my last check. Results sit in two columns, 2027 and new grad roles on the left and other open full-time roles on the right, and saved scores mean each check only pays for roles it has not seen. All 55 automated tests pass with npm test, and my resume and watchlist live in a gitignored folder, so they never reach this public repo.
  • Things learned with AI: Many of the tech and startup companies I target publish their open roles as public JSON through their applicant tracking system, which means no scraping is needed for them. The large corporates on my list mostly do not, which is why they sit in the non-goals for now. I also learned that OpenRouter now returns the dollar cost of every call, so cost per run can be measured rather than guessed. [In the terminal, I learned that every command runs in whichever folder I am in, which I found out the hard way when a failed cd sent a copy command onto my Desktop; chaining steps with && now stops the chain when one fails. I also learned that hidden files such as .gitignore and .env do not show in Finder and are easy to lose in a browser upload, that a .gitignore rule with leading spaces silently stops working, and that .gitignore protects Git pushes but not website uploads.] In Terminus Episodes 1 and 2, I practiced moving around and editing files with pwd, cd, mkdir, echo and rm. The lesson that stuck with me most was that > overwrites a file while >> adds to it, which is a one-character difference between keeping and losing a day's work. Episode 2 then taught me to treat a commit message as a claim and the diff as the truth. Running git show on each commit exposed a "routine ledger upkeep" commit that had quietly rewritten the vault door, which I restored, committed and pushed.
  • What broke / changed in PRD: Keyword scoring ranked a thin real estate analyst posting at 88 while the urban analytics fellowship that fits best scored 36, since overlap rewards short descriptions and cannot read requirements. That moved AI scoring to the center of the PRD and made it the riskiest assumption. The location filter matches city names, so an MBA sourcing internship in Torrance, CA was dropped even though Torrance is in LA County, and I kept a test that documents the gap until I match metro areas instead. Job descriptions also arrive as HTML (Greenhouse encodes it twice), and my first text cleaner left stray spaces that a test caught. Lastly, the first live run surfaced two more problems. At least one of the 40 postings came back with an empty score because Haiku 5.5 spent its whole reply budget thinking, and the filter let roles like "Applied AI Architect, International Policy" through because "Intern" matched part of "International." I added one retry with a larger budget and switched the filter to whole-word matching. The PRD changed twice more before the deadline. I dropped internships, fellowships and pre-docs to focus on full-time roles, and split results into a 2027 and new grad column and an open full-time column, since those are two searches with different timing. I also moved Workday from a non-goal into scope after research showed that about half of the large companies on my list, from NVIDIA to Tishman Speyer, run their careers sites on it. Lastly, my first cost estimate was about three times too low, because my real resume and the longer descriptions from large companies made each prompt bigger than in testing.

Build 2 · Smallest Local Version & PostHog (Due Nov 16)

  • What shipped: Build 2 turned the Build 1 prototype into something a classmate can run without me. A first-run setup screen asks for a resume, the kinds of roles, cities, sectors of companies, schools, "What I'm looking for" and the scoring choice, then saves everything to a private folder, so nobody edits JSON by hand. Resumes can be dropped in as a PDF, Word or text file, and contact details are removed before anything is saved or sent to the model. Each role now comes with three to five LinkedIn search links for alumni, recruiters and the team. The board was redesigned as "The Big Board," with roles sorted into Strong fit, Possible and Long shot tiers. PostHog now runs on this page and in the local app with five named events, and I kept automatic click capture and session recording off, since the app shows my resume and scores. Lastly, "Beyond your list" looks past my watchlist of 238 companies. It checks the public job boards of roughly 9,000 more companies from an open directory (LastRound AI, CC BY 4.0), keeps what passes my filters, and asks the model to score only the top 40. On my own resume, the first run found 3,151 roles that passed, and the best fits were a real estate development analyst role at Atoms and a strategy and operations associate role at Apptronik, both at 72, for $0.03. All 100 automated tests pass with npm test.
  • Things learned with AI: Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby publish jobs as public JSON, but none of them offer a directory of the companies that use them, so the open LastRound dataset of 9,935 companies is what made a search beyond my list possible. A full sweep of those boards pulls about 230,000 open roles in three to five minutes, although running several sweeps in a row slowed one to 37 minutes, which suggests the job boards slow down repeated requests. I also learned that analytics tools can switch on extra tracking from their own project settings. PostHog's dead-click capture would have recorded job titles from my screen until I turned it off in code. Lastly, a push failed because my terminal was signed in to GitHub as my work account while GitHub Desktop used my school account, which is a good reminder that one laptop can hold two identities. [Add anything from this week's class exercises.]
  • What broke / changed in PRD: The PRD changed in three ways this week. First, the user grew from me to any classmate recruiting off-cycle, which meant moving my own defaults, such as real estate and supply chain searches and NYU Stern, out of the code and into choices on the setup screen. Second, the scope grew past a fixed company list. While the watchlist is still the core, the startups I might like most are the ones I do not know to add, so explore now searches beyond it with the same filters. The first free sweep showed how weak keyword ranking is at that scale: only 18 of the top 40 titles named a kind of role I wanted, and "Remote" let in 7,472 roles that were remote only outside the US. After ranking by the role types I chose and treating "Remote" as US remote, 39 of the top 40 matched. Third, the model appears to read the resume rather than keywords, though I found this out by accident. A test resume was left loaded, and every reason cited that person's Microsoft work and flagged an MBA ending in 2028. That supports the riskiest assumption, but it also showed me that a wrong input fails quietly, which is why the board shows the file each check is ranked against. Lastly, one accidental test check on the setup screen spent $0.0175, which is counted below.
